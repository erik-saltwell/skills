# Borrowing Brilliance

Help the user discover concrete possibilities by borrowing mechanisms from other domains. Follow six stages: frame, abstract, choose domains, extract, combine, and synthesize. Complete the last three for one domain at a time, incorporating the user's reaction before exploring the next.

A **solution kernel** is the smallest coherent description of how an idea could solve the framed problem: its organizing mechanism, how it would work in a concrete situation, and the benefit it could create. It is an exploratory possibility, not a specification or validated solution.

## Conversation rhythm

- Start from the context already provided. Read relevant supplied material selectively; do not make the user repeat information or conduct a broad repository scan.
- Interview one focused question at a time. Choose questions that change the frame or unlock exploration. Offer a working interpretation or recommendation when useful, without inventing answers about the user's situation.
- Keep turns conversational and manageable. Label alternatives so the user can refer to them easily. Explain your recommendation and leave room for disagreement.
- Agree on the frame, then the working abstraction, then the borrowing domains before proceeding to dependent work. Use agreement already expressed; do not repeatedly reconfirm settled choices. Follow an explicit request to delegate choices or change the pace.
- Track the current frame, working abstraction, reserve abstractions, selected domains and their exploration status, kernels, and user reactions within the conversation. Distinguish your recommendations from the user's preferences.
- Feedback can change the frame, abstraction, or remaining domains. Briefly explain meaningful changes and revisit only the affected work.

## 1. Frame through an interview

Establish who experiences the problem, a concrete situation in which it occurs, the desired change, and meaningful constraints. Separate observations from assumptions and distinguish an outcome from a proposed solution. If the user starts with an idea, explore the problem or opportunity it addresses without discarding what interests them about it.

Move up to the broader purpose or down to a specific obstacle when the scope seems unhelpful. Ask only enough to establish a useful starting point; this is not exhaustive requirements gathering.

Reflect back a compact frame: **who needs what change, in which situation, despite which obstacle, within which constraints**. Name important uncertainties and refine the frame with the user before abstracting it.

## 2. Propose and refine abstractions

Offer a small set of meaningfully different descriptions of the underlying problem without its domain-specific vocabulary. Preserve the relationships, tensions, and constraints that make the problem difficult. Explain what each abstraction reveals and which kinds of mechanisms it might help us find.

For example, "new team members keep asking where decisions were made" might become "help newcomers recover the context behind existing choices" or "route a question to the right source without interrupting everyone." These invite different borrowing domains; "improve communication" loses too much structure.

Recommend one working abstraction and iterate until the user is satisfied. Keep promising alternatives in reserve. Reopen them when exploration provides a reason, rather than pursuing every abstraction at once.

## 3. Propose and select borrowing domains

Using the working abstraction, propose a manageable set spanning three distances relative to the user's context:

- **Near:** peers or familiar systems handling much the same problem.
- **Adjacent:** another field or activity with a similar structure but different conventions.
- **Far:** a distant setting, such as nature, science, art, games, or history, with a potentially useful structural analogy.

For each candidate, identify the comparable problem, the mechanism or question worth investigating, and why this domain could offer something distinct. A domain name alone is insufficient. Present unverified mechanisms as research leads, not established facts; verify niche factual claims before asserting them.

Recommend a starting set and order, then let the user add, remove, replace, or reorder domains. The proposal should span the three distances; the user's final selection need not satisfy a quota. Research and develop each selected domain as we reach it, rather than presenting kernels for all domains at once.

## 4. Extract a mechanism from the current domain

Perform a short, focused online research pass for a concrete example in this domain. Prefer primary sources or authoritative descriptions that explain how the mechanism works. Follow enough evidence to understand the mechanism and its relevant limitations; avoid turning the session into an exhaustive literature review.

Identify the source problem, the mechanism used to address it, why it works, and the conditions it depends on. Extract relationships, rules, feedback, incentives, or other operative structure rather than surface appearance or vocabulary. Cite links beside the factual claims they support.

Separate sourced observations from your interpretation and proposed adaptation. If sources do not substantiate the mechanism, say so and seek a better example within the domain. If browsing is unavailable, disclose that limit and ask whether to proceed provisionally from clearly labeled hypotheses or wait for source material; do not imply research occurred.

## 5. Combine and translate into the user's context

Map the source mechanism onto the framed problem: which actors, resources, actions, constraints, or relationships correspond? Identify what must change for the mechanism to make sense here, and what should be left behind. Combine the borrowed mechanism with the user's situation, goals, and constraints.

Make the causal connection explicit: **this mechanism could change this behavior or relationship, which could produce this desired effect**. Note the most consequential assumption or mismatch. An analogy helps generate a possibility; evidence that it works in the source domain does not validate the adaptation.

If the analogy is weak, briefly show the mechanism and where the mapping breaks. Recommend moving on and give the user room to identify something worth retaining. Do not force a kernel or keep repairing the analogy unless the user wants to pursue it.

By default, combine each domain's mechanism with the original problem. Combining mechanisms across different borrowing domains is an optional later exploration when the user requests it.

## 6. Synthesize a solution kernel and invite a reaction

For a promising analogy, propose one main kernel. Add a second only when the source suggests a substantially different approach. Extraction, combination, and synthesis can appear together in one concise response; they do not each require a separate confirmation.

Give the user enough to react to:

- **Borrowed mechanism:** the specific example, how it works, and its source.
- **Transfer:** the important correspondences and adaptations to the user's problem.
- **Kernel:** the central idea and a brief scenario showing how someone would experience it and why it might help.
- **Open assumption:** the main uncertainty or place the analogy could break.

Use these as content requirements, not a rigid presentation template. Keep research detail proportionate and make the proposed solution tangible. Avoid feature inventories, implementation plans, rankings, or formal evaluation.

Ask one focused reaction question about the kernel's most meaningful choice, promise, or mismatch, then wait. Refine within this domain if the user wants to; otherwise carry their reaction into the next selected domain. A positive reaction does not automatically require a separate permission question before continuing. If their intent is unclear, ask whether they want to stay with this kernel or explore the next domain.

## Finish with the promising kernels

When the selected domains are explored, or the user wants to stop, give a compact recap of the kernels they found promising. Include each kernel's borrowed mechanism and the user's meaningful reactions or adaptations. Preserve uncertainty and do not label an idea endorsed merely because you proposed it. If none resonated, say so briefly rather than manufacturing a shortlist.

Leave the user to choose what to develop further. Detailed design, systematic evaluation, and cross-domain recombination are separate follow-on work. Keep the session and recap in the conversation; create files or other artifacts only when requested.

## Basis

This is an interactive adaptation inspired by David Kord Murray's *Borrowing Brilliance*, not a reproduction of the book's six stages. Its scope is framing and analogy-based discovery through solution kernels; it does not require simulated subconscious incubation or the book's later judging and enhancing stages. See [the publisher's overview](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/304339/borrowing-brilliance-by-david-kord-murray/) and [Murray's explanation of the method](https://cdn.porchlightbooks.com/legacyassets/system/manifestos/pdfs/000/000/339/original/63.02.BorrowingBrilliance.pdf) for background, not required reading during each session.
